# Keynote Structure — Agentic Hardware Management Platform

Estimated length: 18-25 minutes
Tone: Calm, confident, philosophical. Not a hype talk. More like a thesis defense crossed with a product reveal.

---

## The arc in one sentence

Engineers used to build things. Then they managed people who built things. Now they need to manage agents that build things — and nobody has designed that interface yet.

---

## Slide-by-slide structure

---

### 1. Opening image — no title, no text

A single full-bleed image. A launch pad at dusk, or a rocket on the pad, or a circuit board at extreme macro. No words.

Hold it for 5-8 seconds before you say anything.

**Speaker note:** Silence at the start earns attention. The image should be beautiful and slightly unsettling — something that communicates "what humans can build is extraordinary."

---

### 2. One line

> "Engineering is the most complex thing humans do."

Nothing else on the slide.

**Speaker note:** Don't rush past this. Let the room sit with it. Then pivot: "And we're about to change who does it."

---

### 3. The shift — two columns, minimal

Left: "2015 — Engineers write every line."
Right: "2025 — Agents write most of it."

No bullet points. Just the two statements with a clear visual contrast. Maybe a subtle before/after.

**Speaker note:** Reference Cursor's observation here if relevant — this isn't a prediction, it's already happening. Engineers at major companies are already spending more time reviewing AI output than producing their own. Cite the shift briefly, don't dwell.

---

### 4. The question

> "So what does the engineer do now?"

Full-bleed. One question. Black background.

**Speaker note:** This is the hinge of the whole talk. Everything before this was setup. Everything after is your answer to this question.

---

### 5. The wrong answers — presented fairly

Two common takes:

1. "The engineer disappears." — AI takes over completely, no human needed.
2. "The interface disappears." — No GUI, just prompts into a void.

**Speaker note:** Don't dismiss these cheaply. Steelman them briefly. Then explain why you think they're wrong. The first underestimates the value of human judgment at high stakes. The second confuses "less visible" with "invisible" — people still need to know where they stand when they come back to work.

---

### 6. The analogy

Choose one of these and build a full slide around it:

**Option A — The conductor:** A conductor doesn't play every instrument. They listen, interpret, direct. The orchestra makes sound; the conductor makes music. As the orchestra gets better, the conductor's role doesn't shrink — it becomes more essential.

**Option B — The chief engineer:** At any aerospace company, the chief engineer doesn't write code or run CAD. They review, approve, and hold the vision of the whole. The more capable their team is, the more important their judgment becomes.

**Speaker note:** This is the emotional core of the philosophical argument. Take your time. Use one analogy, not both — one vivid image is stronger than two competing ones.

---

### 7. The thesis — your argument

> "The more agents do, the more human judgment matters. What we need is not a replacement for the engineer — it's a new kind of interface for a new kind of engineer."

**Speaker note:** State this clearly and without hedging. This is your thesis. The product is the proof of concept.

---

### 8. Context — why hardware?

A slide on the stakes. Complex hardware — rockets, robots, nuclear systems — is where the consequences of errors are measured in lives and billions of dollars.

This is the stress test for the argument. If AI-managed engineering works here, it works anywhere.

**Speaker note:** Keep this brief. You don't need to go deep on the hardware engineering world. The point is: this is the hardest version of the problem, which makes it the most interesting place to design for.

---

### 9. The product reveal — first look

Show the UI. A single screenshot or live prototype in its empty state: dark canvas, glassmorphic prompt bar centered on screen, left sidebar, nothing else.

No annotations. Just the image.

**Speaker note:** Don't explain it yet. Let people look at it. Then: "This is what I think that interface looks like."

---

### 10. Walk through the design philosophy — 3 principles

Three slides or three beats on one slide:

**1. The canvas, not the chat.**
It's not a chatbot. The user isn't waiting for a reply — they're overseeing a process. The space needs to feel like a control room, not a messaging app.

**2. Visible without overwhelming.**
The node graph shows everything the agent is doing, but it's structured. You can scan it in 2 seconds or drill in for an hour. The opacity gradient on old actions means the screen never becomes a wall of text.

**3. The approval as a first-class moment.**
Most products treat confirmation dialogs as friction to minimize. This product treats the approval moment as the most important interaction — it's where the human earns their place in the loop. It should feel weighty, not like clicking "OK."

---

### 11. The demo

Live or recorded — walk through the prototype from prompt submission to the investigate panel.

Suggested narration beats:
- Submit the prompt. Watch the bar move.
- "The agent starts. You don't see code running or files opening. You see intent."
- Watch the branches grow. Point out the parallel execution.
- "CAD, simulation, documentation — simultaneously. Three engineers' worth of work, visible in one view."
- The amber node appears. Pause here.
- "And then something needs you."
- Click investigate. Let the screen split animation breathe.
- "This is the moment the product is built around."
- Approve. Watch the graph complete.

**Speaker note:** Resist the urge to narrate every node as it appears. Let silence do the work while the graph grows. Talk when it matters.

---

### 12. The name and its meaning

Introduce the product name here — after the demo, not before.

Put the name on screen with its philosophical etymology underneath. One or two sentences on why the name reflects the philosophy.

Example if the name is Nous:
> "Nous — ancient Greek for the organizing mind. The intelligence that sets everything in motion while remaining itself unmixed."

**Speaker note:** The name landing after the demo means the audience has already *felt* the product. Now you're giving them language for it. That order matters.

---

### 13. The bigger vision

Pull back. This isn't just about rockets.

Every domain where stakes are high and complexity is growing — medicine, infrastructure, climate, defense — is going to face this same question. What does the human interface look like when AI is doing the work?

This product is a proposed answer. One answer. But it's a direction worth exploring.

**Speaker note:** Don't overreach here. You're not claiming to have solved everything. You're claiming to have asked the right question and taken a genuine first step at answering it.

---

### 14. Close — the question again

Return to the slide from beat 4:

> "So what does the engineer do now?"

Let it sit. Then:

> "They oversee. They judge. They approve. And they need an interface worthy of that responsibility."

Fade to black or to the product name.

---

## Structural notes

**Do not open with context.** The talk should start with image and feeling, not "AI is changing everything" — everyone knows that. Open with what's specific and yours.

**The demo is the peak, not the close.** End on the philosophical argument — bring it back up after the demo so people leave thinking about the idea, not just the interface.

**Slides should be mostly image and negative space.** If a slide has more than one sentence of text, it's probably two slides. The words go in your mouth, not on the screen.

**The approve/investigate split is your money moment.** If you're short on time, cut from elsewhere — do not cut or rush past that interaction. That's what people will remember.
